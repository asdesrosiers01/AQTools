Comparing a model to the standard: individual years vs. a 5-year run

Every NAAQS form is either a multi-year average of an annual statistic (NO₂ 1-hr, SO₂ 1-hr, O₃ 8-hr, PM2.5 annual, PM2.5 24-hr — all averaged over 3 years) or a not-to-be-exceeded form evaluated against the single highest year (CO, PM10, Pb). 40 CFR Part 51 Appendix W §9.2.2(a)–(b) sets the overall framework: the design concentration compared to the NAAQS is the background concentration (§8.3) plus the modeled source impact, and the specific NAAQS form governs how those are combined — but Appendix W explicitly does not spell out the year-by-year mechanics itself, pointing instead to "applicable modeling guidance available on the EPA's SCRAM website" (§9.2.2(b)). In practice, that guidance is pollutant/standard-specific EPA memoranda, most directly the March 1, 2011 memo Additional Clarification Regarding Application of Appendix W Modeling Guidance for the 1-hour NO2 NAAQS, which states the general rule EPA has since applied to the other 3-year-average forms as well:

  • 3-year-average forms, run with 5 years of NWS meteorological data (the preferred minimum per §8.4.2(e)): pull the relevant annual statistic from each of the 5 modeled years and average all 5 directly. EPA's 2011 NO₂ memo is explicit that this "serves as an unbiased estimate of the 3-year average," and that modeling "rolling 3-year averages" (years 1–3, 2–4, 3–5, taking the highest) "is not required." A generic multi-year average is the default; a previous version of this page stated the rolling-3-year/highest-window approach as if it were required, which this EPA memo directly contradicts — corrected here.
  • 3-year-average forms, run with fewer years: with exactly 3 years of data, average those 3. With only 1 year of site-specific data (the regulatory minimum), that single year's statistic is used directly as a less-certain surrogate design value.
  • Not-to-be-exceeded forms (CO, PM10, Pb): use the single highest modeled concentration across the full modeled period ("H1H", first-highest), with no averaging across years.

Regional and state permitting authorities sometimes layer their own implementation guidance on top of this (e.g. requiring a rolling-3-year review anyway, or specifying which years of met data to use); confirm the applicable authority's current modeling guidance before finalizing a design value calculation — this page states the federal default, not every jurisdiction's overlay on it.

Significant Impact Levels (SILs)

A SIL is a small concentration threshold used to screen a proposed source out of a full NAAQS/PSD-increment cumulative impact analysis: model the project alone first, and if its impact is below the SIL everywhere, a cumulative analysis (adding background and other sources) generally isn't required. Some SILs are codified; others are EPA guidance a reviewing authority may or may not follow — this table says which is which.

Ozone has no codified SIL at all: it's a secondary pollutant (formed from precursor emissions, not emitted directly), so any ozone significant-impact screening is inherently a modeling-guidance construct rather than a NAAQS/PSD regulatory requirement. The PM2.5 annual SIL has been revised twice since the original 2010 rule (0.3 → 0.2 in 2018 guidance → 0.13 in 2024 guidance, the last tied to the 2024 NAAQS revision) without the codified regulatory text at 40 CFR 51.165(b)(2) ever being updated to match — that regulation still says 0.3 µg/m³. Confirm which value your reviewing authority expects before relying on the newer guidance figure. SILs above are the Class II (general/default) values used outside Class I areas; Class I areas carry their own, smaller SIL values for the increments where they've been established, which this table does not include — see the Class I Areas tool for the areas themselves. Background concentrations are not part of a SIL comparison; a source below the SIL can still combine with background to exceed the NAAQS, which is exactly why a cumulative analysis remains the fallback when the SIL screen doesn't clear.

Background concentrations

A modeled concentration from a single source is compared against the NAAQS only after adding a background concentration representing everything else contributing to ambient levels — other sources, transport, natural background. 40 CFR Part 51 Appendix W §8.3 and EPA's supporting guidance describe two general approaches:

Monitored design value approach

Use a single, constant background value equal to the design value (computed the same way as the NAAQS form itself, e.g. the 3-year-averaged 98th percentile) from a monitor that is regionally representative of the source's surroundings and not itself dominated by the source being permitted. This is the simpler approach and the default where a suitably representative monitor exists.

Seasonal (temporally-varying) adjustment approach

Instead of one constant value, develop background concentrations that vary by month, season, or hour-of-day/day-of-week, built from the same representative monitor's data binned accordingly (e.g. a monthly average of the relevant percentile, or hour-of-day averages for pollutants such as NO₂ and CO with strong diurnal patterns tied to traffic and heating load). This pairs each modeled hour or day with a background value from the matching period rather than a single worst-case constant, which EPA guidance favors when it materially changes the outcome and sufficient monitor data exist to support the finer binning. It requires more monitor data and more justification of representativeness than the constant-value approach, and the permitting authority's concurrence on the binning scheme before it's relied on in a submission.

What this page doesn't do

It doesn't calculate a design value from your model output, doesn't select or validate a representative monitor, and doesn't know which of these two background approaches your permitting authority prefers or requires for a given pollutant and location — that varies by EPA region and state program, and is worth confirming before a submission is built around a choice made here.
